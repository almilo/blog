*Part 2 of the series "Software that knows".*

## Recap

The [hypothesis](https://almilo.com/blog/software-that-knows-the-hypothesis/):
capture an organisation's knowledge, make it formal and explicit, and put it
at the centre of its business applications.

## Prior work

The idea isn't new. Expert systems in the 1980s and model-driven development
in the 2000s aimed at something similar.

- Both stalled, in large part because formalising knowledge was slow and
  expensive.
- It needed specialists, such as knowledge engineers and ontologists, to
  interview experts and model what they knew.
- Expert-systems research had a name for it: the knowledge acquisition
  bottleneck.

## What changed

LLMs and agents lower the cost of the first draft.

- Turning documents and conversations into structured knowledge is now much
  faster and cheaper.
- For example, a first draft of a small ontology can be extracted from an
  internal SOP in one working session, without a dedicated ontologist.
- Even with review, the total effort is a fraction of what it used to be.

## What didn't change

- **The draft isn't the result.** It follows a familiar 80/20 split: LLM
  extraction covers roughly 80% of the content in 20% of the time. Review and
  acceptance by the people who own the knowledge take the other 80% of the
  time, for the last 20%: exceptions, edge cases and unwritten rules.
- **Review is needed for adoption.** People tend to trust and use rules they
  helped shape.
- **Review is needed for compliance.** Formal knowledge that drives business
  applications has to be checked before it's relied on.
- **Accountability stays with people.** LLMs are tools, not owners: an LLM
  can't answer for a decision, bear its consequences or be held liable.
  Regulation points the same way; the EU AI Act, for example, requires human
  oversight of high-risk AI systems.

## How we'd know it's wrong

- Human review costs more than LLM extraction saves.
- Extraction quality doesn't hold beyond small, well-written documents.
